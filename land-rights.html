Property and land

The law on ownership and possession of immovable property, partition of family property, succession, agreements for sale and compensation for land acquired by the State, with notes on the position in Kerala.

Law at a glance

Statutes: Transfer of Property Act, 1882; Registration Act, 1908; Specific Relief Act, 1963; Limitation Act, 1963; Code of Civil Procedure, 1908; Hindu Succession Act, 1956; Indian Succession Act, 1925; Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (RFCTLARR Act).
Forums: civil courts (Munsiff and Sub Courts according to the value of the suit); the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority for acquisition references.

Title and revenue records

Ownership of land comes from title: a registered sale, gift, settlement, partition or release deed, a will, or inheritance under the law of succession. A sale of immovable property worth one hundred rupees or more can be made only by a registered instrument (Section 54, Transfer of Property Act; Section 17, Registration Act).

Mutation is not title

Entries in revenue records, such as mutation (pokkuvaravu), the thandaper register and land tax receipts, are maintained for fiscal purposes. They do not create or extinguish title (Suraj Bhan v. Financial Commissioner, 2007). They may be relevant evidence of possession, but a dispute about title has to be decided by a civil court.

Transfers by “sale agreement, general power of attorney and will” do not convey title to immovable property. Title passes only by a registered deed of conveyance (Suraj Lamp & Industries v. State of Haryana, 2011).

Possession and injunctions

Recovery of possession after dispossession (Section 6, Specific Relief Act)

A person dispossessed of immovable property without consent and otherwise than in due course of law may sue to recover possession within six months of dispossession. The court decides only the fact of prior possession and dispossession, not title. No such suit lies against the Government. A suit based on title can still be filed later.

Injunction or declaration?

Where the plaintiff’s possession is clear and title is not seriously in dispute, a suit for injunction alone may be enough. Where title is disputed or there is a cloud on it, the plaintiff should sue for a declaration of title, with possession or an injunction as consequential relief (Anathula Sudhakar v. P. Buchi Reddy, 2008).

Boundary disputes

Boundaries can be surveyed and demarcated by the Survey department under the Kerala Survey and Boundaries Act, 1961. Where the dispute continues, the civil court decides it, usually after appointing an advocate commissioner and a surveyor to measure the property.

Partition and succession

Any co-owner may ask for the common property to be divided. If the co-owners do not agree on a partition deed, a suit for partition is filed. The court first passes a preliminary decree declaring the shares, and then a final decree dividing the property by metes and bounds, usually with the help of a commissioner.

Daughters’ rights in Hindu families

Under Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, as amended in 2005, a daughter is a coparcener by birth in the same manner as a son. In Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020), the Supreme Court held that this right does not depend on whether the father was alive on 9 September 2005.

Kerala: July 2025 ruling

Kerala abolished the joint family system by the Kerala Joint Hindu Family System (Abolition) Act, 1975. In July 2025, the Kerala High Court held that Sections 3 and 4 of that Act are repugnant to Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act as amended in 2005, and cannot prevail over it. Daughters in Kerala are therefore entitled to equal shares in ancestral property, subject to Section 6(5), which protects partitions effected before 20 December 2004. Check the current position, including any appeal, before relying on this ruling.

Other communities

  • Christians: succession is governed by the Indian Succession Act, 1925. In Mary Roy v. State of Kerala (1986), the Supreme Court held that the Travancore Christian Succession Act had ceased to apply, giving Christian daughters in the former Travancore area equal rights with sons.
  • Muslims: succession is governed by Muslim personal law, under which shares are fixed by law. A Muslim may bequeath by will no more than one-third of the estate, unless the heirs consent.

Wills and family arrangements

A person propounding a will must prove that it was duly executed and attested, and must remove any suspicious circumstances surrounding it (H. Venkatachala Iyengar v. B.N. Thimmajamma, 1958). A bona fide family settlement that resolves disputes among family members is upheld by the courts. A memorandum recording a settlement already made does not require registration (Kale v. Deputy Director of Consolidation, 1976).

Adverse possession

A person who has been in open, continuous and hostile possession of land, to the knowledge of the true owner, for 12 years (30 years against the Government) can acquire title by adverse possession. In Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur (2019), a three-judge bench held that such a person may also file a suit to declare that title, not only use it as a defence. Mere long possession, or possession with the owner’s permission, is not enough.

Agreements for sale

  • An agreement for sale does not by itself create any interest in the property. The buyer’s remedy, if the seller defaults, is a suit for specific performance.
  • Since the Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018, specific performance is the general rule rather than a discretionary relief, though the buyer must still show readiness and willingness to perform.
  • The suit must be filed within three years from the date fixed for performance, or, if no date is fixed, from the date the buyer has notice that performance is refused (Article 54, Limitation Act).
  • For immovable property, time is not ordinarily of the essence, but the contract must be performed within a reasonable time (K.S. Vidyanadam v. Vairavan, 1997).

Land acquisition compensation

When land is acquired for a public purpose, compensation is determined under the RFCTLARR Act, 2013. The right to property is a constitutional right under Article 300A, and no one can be deprived of property save by authority of law (Vidya Devi v. State of Himachal Pradesh, 2020).

How compensation is built up
  1. Market value (Section 26): the highest of (a) the value fixed for stamp duty, (b) the average sale price of similar land in the vicinity, based on the higher-priced half of sale deeds over the preceding three years, and (c) the consented amount in acquisitions for private companies or public-private partnerships.
  2. Multiplication factor (First Schedule): 1 for urban areas; between 1 and 2 for rural areas, depending on the distance from the urban area, as notified by the State Government.
  3. Value of assets attached to the land, such as buildings, trees and crops.
  4. Solatium (Section 30(1)): 100% of the compensation amount.
  5. Additional amount (Section 30(3)): 12% per year on the market value, from the date of the notification of the social impact assessment study to the date of the award or of taking possession, whichever is earlier.
Illustration

Rural land with a market value of ₹10 lakh and a notified multiplication factor of 2: ₹10 lakh × 2 = ₹20 lakh; solatium of 100% = ₹20 lakh; compensation = ₹40 lakh, plus the value of structures and trees and the 12% additional amount on the market value.

Rehabilitation and resettlement

Affected families are also entitled to the rehabilitation and resettlement benefits in the Second Schedule. These include a choice of employment, a one-time payment of ₹5 lakh or an annuity; a subsistence allowance of ₹3,000 per month for one year for displaced families; and transportation and resettlement allowances.

Challenging the award

A person who does not accept the Collector’s award may apply to the Collector, within the time allowed by Section 64, for a reference to the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority. Interest on delayed payment of compensation is payable at 9% per year for the first year and 15% per year after that (Section 80).

Documents usually required

  • Title deeds and prior title deeds (at least 30 years)
  • Encumbrance certificate
  • Possession certificate and thandaper extract
  • Latest land tax receipt
  • Location sketch and survey plan
  • Building permit and tax receipts, if built upon
  • Legal heirship certificate and family tree, in partition matters
  • Acquisition notifications and the award, in acquisition matters

Key judgments

Daughters as coparceners

Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma, Supreme Court, 2020

A daughter is a coparcener by birth with the same rights as a son, whether or not her father was alive on 9 September 2005.

Christian daughters’ succession

Mary Roy v. State of Kerala, Supreme Court, 1986

The Indian Succession Act, 1925, and not the Travancore Christian Succession Act, governs intestate succession among Christians in the former Travancore area.

Revenue entries do not confer title

Suraj Bhan v. Financial Commissioner, Supreme Court, 2007

Mutation entries are only for fiscal purposes and do not create or extinguish title.

GPA and agreement sales

Suraj Lamp & Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana, Supreme Court, 2011

Transactions by sale agreement, general power of attorney and will are not transfers of title; title passes only by a registered conveyance.

Adverse possession as a basis for a suit

Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur, Supreme Court, 2019

A person who has perfected title by adverse possession can sue on it, and need not wait to be sued.

Suits for injunction and declaration

Anathula Sudhakar v. P. Buchi Reddy, Supreme Court, 2008

Explained when a bare injunction suit is enough and when a declaration of title must be sought.

Time in contracts for sale of land

K.S. Vidyanadam v. Vairavan, Supreme Court, 1997

Time is not ordinarily of the essence in contracts relating to immovable property, but the buyer must act within a reasonable time.

Proof of a will

H. Venkatachala Iyengar v. B.N. Thimmajamma, Supreme Court, 1958

The propounder of a will must prove due execution and remove any suspicious circumstances.

Family settlements

Kale v. Deputy Director of Consolidation, Supreme Court, 1976

Bona fide family arrangements are favoured by the courts and are binding on the parties.

Right to property

Vidya Devi v. State of Himachal Pradesh, Supreme Court, 2020

Taking private land without following due process and paying compensation violates Article 300A.

Lapse of old acquisitions

Indore Development Authority v. Manoharlal, Supreme Court (Constitution Bench), 2020

Explained when acquisitions begun under the 1894 Act lapse under Section 24(2) of the 2013 Act.
