Family and matrimonial law

Divorce, maintenance, custody of children and protection from domestic violence under the personal laws and the general law, and how matters proceed before the Family Court.

Law at a glance

Personal laws: Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 (Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs); Muslim personal law, the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986 and the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019; Indian Divorce Act, 1869 (Christians); Special Marriage Act, 1954.
General laws: Family Courts Act, 1984; Guardians and Wards Act, 1890; Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956; Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005; Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.

New criminal laws

From 1 July 2024, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS) replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) replaced the Indian Penal Code. Maintenance under Section 125 CrPC is now Section 144 BNSS, and cruelty by a husband or his relatives under Section 498A IPC is now Section 85 BNS.

Divorce

Divorce by mutual consent (Section 13B, Hindu Marriage Act)

  • Husband and wife file a joint petition stating that they have been living separately for one year or more, cannot live together, and have agreed that the marriage should be dissolved.
  • The second motion is ordinarily made not earlier than six months and not later than 18 months after the petition is filed.
  • The six-month period is not mandatory. The Family Court may waive it where the conditions in Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur (2017) are met. The Supreme Court may also dissolve the marriage directly under Article 142 (Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan, 2023).
  • Terms on maintenance, permanent alimony, custody of children and return of property are usually recorded in the settlement.

Contested divorce (Section 13, Hindu Marriage Act)

Either spouse may seek divorce on grounds including adultery, cruelty, desertion for a continuous period of at least two years, conversion to another religion, incurable unsoundness of mind or mental disorder, venereal disease in a communicable form, renunciation of the world, and not being heard of as alive for seven years. A wife has certain additional grounds under Section 13(2).

Under Section 13(1A), either party may also seek divorce if there has been no resumption of cohabitation for one year after a decree of judicial separation, or no restitution of conjugal rights for one year after a decree for restitution.

Other communities

  • Christians: divorce is governed by the Indian Divorce Act, 1869, as amended in 2001. In Ammini E.J. v. Union of India (1995), the Kerala High Court struck down the discriminatory requirement that a Christian wife prove adultery together with another ground.
  • Muslims: divorce may be by talaq (other than instant triple talaq, which is void and an offence under the 2019 Act), khula, mubara’at, or by a court decree under the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939.
  • Inter-faith and registered marriages: the Special Marriage Act, 1954 applies.

Maintenance

Maintenance can be claimed under more than one law. The court takes into account amounts already awarded in other proceedings.

  • Section 144 BNSS (formerly Section 125 CrPC): a wife unable to maintain herself, children and parents can claim monthly maintenance from a person who has sufficient means and neglects or refuses to maintain them. The remedy is available to women of all religions, including divorced Muslim women (Mohd. Abdul Samad v. State of Telangana, 2024).
  • Hindu Marriage Act: maintenance during the proceedings and litigation expenses (Section 24), and permanent alimony (Section 25).
  • Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956: a Hindu wife’s right to maintenance while living separately for valid reasons (Section 18).
  • Domestic Violence Act: monetary relief under Section 20.
  • Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986: a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance from the former husband (Danial Latifi v. Union of India, 2001).
Rajnesh v. Neha (2020)

The Supreme Court directed that both parties in every maintenance case file an affidavit disclosing their assets and liabilities, and that maintenance be awarded from the date of the application. It also listed the factors for fixing the amount: the status of the parties, reasonable needs, the income and liabilities of both, and the standard of living during the marriage.

Custody and guardianship of children

  • In every custody dispute, the welfare of the child is the paramount consideration, above the rights or wishes of either parent.
  • Under Section 6 of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, the father and, after him, the mother is the natural guardian, but custody of a child below five years is ordinarily with the mother. In Githa Hariharan v. Reserve Bank of India (1999), the Supreme Court held that the mother can act as natural guardian even while the father is alive, for example when he is not in charge of the child’s affairs.
  • Custody and visitation are decided by the Family Court, mainly under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890. The parent without custody is usually given visitation rights, and the court may consider the preference of a child old enough to form an intelligent preference.

Protection from domestic violence

The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 protects a woman who lives, or has lived, in a domestic relationship, including a marriage or a relationship in the nature of marriage, from physical, sexual, verbal, emotional and economic abuse. Applications are made to the Magistrate, directly or through a Protection Officer. The reliefs include:

  • protection orders restraining acts of violence and contact (Section 18);
  • residence orders securing the right to live in the shared household (Section 19);
  • monetary relief for expenses and losses, including maintenance (Section 20);
  • temporary custody of children (Section 21); and
  • compensation for injuries, including mental torture and emotional distress (Section 22).

A “shared household” can include a house owned by the husband’s relatives in which the woman lived in a domestic relationship (Satish Chander Ahuja v. Sneha Ahuja, 2020).

Stridhan

Stridhan, the property given to a woman before, at or after her marriage, belongs absolutely to her. A husband or in-laws who hold it hold it in trust, and refusing to return it can amount to criminal breach of trust (Pratibha Rani v. Suraj Kumar, 1985). The offence was under Section 406 IPC and is now under Section 316 BNS.

Family Court procedure

  • Settlement first: the Family Court must first try to help the parties reach a settlement (Section 9, Family Courts Act). Matters are often referred to counselling or mediation.
  • Privacy: proceedings may be held in camera if the court so wishes, and must be if either party asks (Section 11).
  • Representation: parties are not entitled as of right to be represented by a lawyer, but the court may permit it (Section 13).
  • Appeals: an appeal from a Family Court judgment lies to the High Court.

Documents usually required

  • Marriage certificate or other proof of marriage
  • Birth certificates of children
  • Proof of address of both parties
  • Income proof, bank statements and tax returns
  • List of stridhan and other property, with evidence
  • Correspondence, messages and photographs relevant to the dispute
  • Medical records and police complaints, if any
  • Copies of earlier orders in related cases

Key judgments

Waiver of the cooling-off period

Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, Supreme Court, 2017

The six-month period under Section 13B(2) is directory, not mandatory, and the court may waive it where there is no chance of reconciliation and the parties have settled their differences.

Irretrievable breakdown

Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan, Supreme Court (Constitution Bench), 2023

The Supreme Court may, under Article 142, dissolve a marriage that has irretrievably broken down, and may dispense with the waiting period.

Maintenance guidelines

Rajnesh v. Neha, Supreme Court, 2020

Required both parties to file affidavits of assets and liabilities, and held that maintenance runs from the date of the application.

Maintenance for Muslim women

Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum, Supreme Court, 1985; Mohd. Abdul Samad v. State of Telangana, Supreme Court, 2024

The secular remedy of maintenance under Section 125 CrPC (now Section 144 BNSS) is available to Muslim women, including divorced women, alongside the remedies under the 1986 Act.

The 1986 Act upheld

Danial Latifi v. Union of India, Supreme Court, 2001

Upheld the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986, reading it to require a reasonable and fair provision for the divorced wife’s future, to be made within the iddat period.

Instant triple talaq

Shayara Bano v. Union of India, Supreme Court, 2017

Instant and irrevocable triple talaq (talaq-e-biddat) was set aside. Parliament later made it void and an offence by the 2019 Act.

Shared household

Satish Chander Ahuja v. Sneha Ahuja, Supreme Court, 2020

A woman’s right of residence under the Domestic Violence Act may extend to a house owned by her in-laws in which she lived.

Relationships in the nature of marriage

D. Velusamy v. D. Patchaiammal, Supreme Court, 2010

Explained when a live-in relationship is a “relationship in the nature of marriage” for the purposes of the Domestic Violence Act.

Stridhan

Pratibha Rani v. Suraj Kumar, Supreme Court, 1985

Stridhan is the absolute property of the wife; its retention by the husband or in-laws can amount to criminal breach of trust.

Mother as natural guardian

Githa Hariharan v. Reserve Bank of India, Supreme Court, 1999

Read “after” in Section 6(a) of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act to mean “in the absence of”, so that the mother can act as natural guardian during the father’s lifetime.

Christian divorce grounds

Ammini E.J. v. Union of India, Kerala High Court (Full Bench), 1995

Struck down the requirement in Section 10 of the Indian Divorce Act that a Christian wife must prove adultery coupled with another ground, while a husband had only to prove adultery.

Right to marry a person of one’s choice

Lata Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh, Supreme Court, 2006

An adult is free to marry a person of their choice, and the police must protect inter-caste and inter-religious couples from harassment and violence.
