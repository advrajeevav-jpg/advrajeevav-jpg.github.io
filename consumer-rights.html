Consumer protection

Rights under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, what counts as a defect, deficiency or unfair trade practice, and how a complaint is filed and decided before the Consumer Commissions.

Law at a glance

Statute: Consumer Protection Act, 2019 (in force from 20 July 2020), which replaced the Consumer Protection Act, 1986; Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020.
Forums: District, State and National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions; the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) for misleading advertisements and unfair trade practices affecting consumers as a class.

Who is a consumer

Under Section 2(7), a consumer is a person who buys goods or hires or avails of services for consideration, whether paid, promised or partly paid. This includes transactions made online, through teleshopping or by multi-level marketing. It also covers anyone who uses the goods or services with the buyer’s approval.

A person who buys goods for resale or for a commercial purpose is not a consumer. Goods bought and used by a person exclusively to earn a livelihood by self-employment are not treated as bought for a commercial purpose.

The six consumer rights (Section 2(9))

  • to be protected against hazardous goods, products and services;
  • to be informed about the quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard and price of goods and services;
  • to be assured of access to a variety of goods and services at competitive prices;
  • to be heard and to have consumer interests considered at appropriate forums;
  • to seek redressal against unfair or restrictive trade practices and unscrupulous exploitation; and
  • to consumer awareness.

Defect, deficiency and unfair trade practice

TermMeaningExamples
Defect (goods)Any fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard of goods.A vehicle with a manufacturing defect; an appliance that fails repeatedly within warranty.
Deficiency (services)Any fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality, nature and manner of performance of a service, including negligence or deliberate withholding of relevant information.Delay in handing over a flat; wrongful rejection of an insurance claim; unauthorised bank debits; medical negligence in paid treatment.
Unfair trade practiceA deceptive or unfair method of promoting sale, use or supply.False claims about quality; refusing to take back defective goods or refund the price within the period stated in the bill, or within 30 days where no period is stated.

How to file a complaint

  1. Notice to the seller or service provider

    A written notice demanding a remedy is not compulsory under the Act, but it often brings a settlement and is useful evidence. The National Consumer Helpline (1915) can also help resolve grievances before a complaint is filed.

  2. Choose the right Commission

    Territorial jurisdiction (Section 34): the complaint may be filed where the opposite party resides or carries on business or has a branch office, where the cause of action arose, or where the complainant resides or personally works for gain.

  3. File the complaint

    The complaint may be filed in person or online through e-Jagriti, the online platform of the Department of Consumer Affairs. A complainant may appear in person; engaging an advocate is optional.

  4. Admission, reply and evidence

    The Commission decides whether to admit the complaint, issues notice to the opposite party, receives its version and the evidence of both sides, usually on affidavit, and may refer the dispute to mediation where both parties agree.

  5. Order

    The Commission may order repair, replacement or refund, compensation for loss and injury, punitive damages in appropriate cases, discontinuance of the unfair practice, and costs.

Pecuniary jurisdiction

Since 2021, pecuniary jurisdiction depends on the value of the consideration paid for the goods or services, not on the amount of compensation claimed.

Time limit and fees

  • A complaint must be filed within two years from the date on which the cause of action arose (Section 69). Delay may be condoned if sufficient cause is shown.
  • No fee is payable for complaints where the value of the goods or services paid is up to ₹5 lakh. Above that, a graded fee applies.

Appeals

  • From the District Commission to the State Commission: within 45 days (Section 41).
  • From the State Commission to the National Commission: within 30 days (Section 51).
  • From the National Commission to the Supreme Court: within 30 days (Section 67).

An appeal against an order directing payment requires a deposit of a part of the amount awarded, as the Act provides.

Product liability (Sections 82 to 87)

A consumer harmed by a defective product can claim compensation from the product manufacturer, the product service provider or the product seller.

  • Manufacturer: liable for a manufacturing defect, a design defect, deviation from specifications, non-conformity with an express warranty, or inadequate instructions or warnings. Under Section 84(2), a manufacturer is liable even if it proves that it was not negligent or fraudulent in making an express warranty.
  • Seller: liable in specified cases, for example where the seller exercised substantial control over the design, testing, packaging or labelling, altered the product, made an express warranty, or where the manufacturer cannot be identified or served.
  • E-commerce: e-commerce entities have duties under the E-Commerce Rules, 2020, and an online platform may itself be liable where it acts as a product seller.

Everyday consumer issues

“No exchange, no refund” notices

Refusing to take back defective goods, or to refund the price within the period stated in the bill (or within 30 days if no period is stated), is an unfair trade practice under the 2019 Act, whatever the notice at the counter says.

Charging for carry bags

Consumer Commissions have held that charging customers for branded carry bags is an unfair trade practice. For example, the District Consumer Forum at Chandigarh so held in Dinesh Prasad Raturi v. Bata India Ltd. (2019).

Selling above MRP

A retailer cannot sell a pre-packaged commodity above its MRP. In Federation of Hotel & Restaurant Associations of India v. Union of India (2017), the Supreme Court held that hotels and restaurants serving bottled water as part of their service are not bound by the MRP.

Restaurant service charge

The CCPA’s guidelines of July 2022 bar hotels and restaurants from adding a service charge to the bill automatically or by default. Any service charge must be voluntary and at the customer’s discretion.

Documents to keep ready

  • Bill, invoice or receipt of payment
  • Warranty or guarantee card
  • Agreement, brochure or advertisement relied on
  • Job cards and service requests
  • Emails, letters and chat records with the seller
  • Photographs or expert reports showing the defect
  • Copy of any notice sent and the reply
  • Proof of loss or expenses suffered

Key judgments

Medical services are covered

Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha, Supreme Court, 1995

Services rendered by medical practitioners and hospitals for a fee are “services” under consumer law; free services are generally outside it.

Proof of medical negligence

V. Kishan Rao v. Nikhil Super Speciality Hospital, Supreme Court, 2010

Expert evidence is not required in every medical negligence complaint; the Commission decides on the facts whether expert opinion is necessary.

Housing authorities

Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. Gupta, Supreme Court, 1993

Statutory housing and development authorities provide “services” and are answerable before consumer forums for delay and deficiency, including compensation for harassment.

One-sided builder agreements

Pioneer Urban Land & Infrastructure Ltd. v. Govindan Raghavan, Supreme Court, 2019

One-sided clauses in builder-buyer agreements are an unfair trade practice and do not bind the buyer, who may seek a refund with interest on inordinate delay.

Compensation for delayed possession

Wg. Cdr. Arifur Rahman Khan v. DLF Southern Homes Pvt. Ltd., Supreme Court, 2020

Flat buyers who choose to take possession despite delay are still entitled to compensation for the delay.

Recovery agents

ICICI Bank Ltd. v. Shanti Devi Sharma, Supreme Court, 2008

The Supreme Court deprecated the use of force and intimidation by banks and their recovery agents to recover loans.

Electricity supply is a service

Karnataka Power Transmission Corporation v. Ashok Iron Works Pvt. Ltd., Supreme Court, 2009

Supply of electricity is a “service”, and a consumer of electricity may approach the consumer forum for deficiency.

Insurance exclusions

Texco Marketing Pvt. Ltd. v. TATA AIG General Insurance Co. Ltd., Supreme Court, 2022

An insurer relying on an exclusion clause must show that it was clearly disclosed to the insured.

MRP and restaurants

Federation of Hotel & Restaurant Associations of India v. Union of India, Supreme Court, 2017

Hotels and restaurants serving packaged water as part of their service are not making a retail sale, and are not bound by the MRP.
