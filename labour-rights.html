Labour and employment

The rights of workers and the duties of employers under the four Labour Codes that came into force on 21 November 2025: termination and disputes, wages, gratuity, maternity benefit, compensation for workplace injuries and protection against sexual harassment.

The law has changed

From 21 November 2025, four Labour Codes replaced 29 central labour laws, including the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, the Employees’ Compensation Act, 1923 and the Maternity Benefit Act, 1961. Central and State rules under the Codes are being notified in stages. Disputes and claims that arose under the earlier Acts may continue to be governed by them, as the savings provisions of the Codes allow. Judgments under the earlier Acts remain useful guidance where the Codes carry forward the same principles.

Termination and retrenchment

A worker who has been in continuous service for at least one year cannot be retrenched unless the employer:

  • gives one month’s written notice stating the reasons, or pays wages for the notice period in lieu of notice;
  • pays retrenchment compensation equal to 15 days’ average pay for every completed year of continuous service; and
  • gives notice to the appropriate Government in the prescribed manner.

Under the Industrial Relations Code, establishments with 300 or more workers (100 under the earlier Act) need the prior permission of the appropriate Government for lay-off, retrenchment and closure.

Dismissal for misconduct

Dismissal for misconduct must follow a fair domestic inquiry consistent with the certified or model standing orders and the principles of natural justice. A dismissal found to be illegal may result in reinstatement with back wages, or compensation in lieu of reinstatement.

Fixed-term employment

The Codes recognise fixed-term employment. A fixed-term employee must get the same hours of work, wages, allowances and benefits as a permanent worker doing the same or similar work, and becomes eligible for gratuity on a proportionate basis after one year of continuous service.

Raising an industrial dispute

  1. Representation to the employer

    Raise the grievance in writing with the employer, and with the Grievance Redressal Committee where one exists.

  2. Conciliation

    The dispute is raised before the Conciliation Officer of the Labour Department, who tries to bring about a settlement. A settlement in conciliation binds the parties.

  3. Industrial Tribunal

    If conciliation fails, the dispute may be taken to the Industrial Tribunal, as the Industrial Relations Code provides. The Code replaces the earlier system of Labour Courts and Industrial Tribunals with Industrial Tribunals. Awards are enforceable.

Time limits

The Codes prescribe time limits for raising disputes and making claims. For example, a claim for employee’s compensation must be made within two years of the accident or death. Act promptly and check the limit that applies to your claim.

Wages

  • Minimum wages now apply to all employees, not only to scheduled employments. The Central Government fixes a floor wage, and State minimum wages cannot be lower than it.
  • Timely payment: monthly wages are to be paid before the expiry of the seventh day of the following month.
  • No discrimination on the ground of gender in wages, or in recruitment for the same or similar work.
  • Definition of “wages”: wages broadly mean basic pay, dearness allowance and retaining allowance. If the excluded allowances exceed half of the total remuneration, the excess is treated as wages. This affects the calculation of gratuity and other benefits.

Gratuity

Under the Code on Social Security, gratuity is payable on termination of employment after at least five years of continuous service, whether by superannuation, retirement, resignation, death or disablement. The five-year condition does not apply on death or disablement. Fixed-term employees qualify after one year.

Calculation (monthly-rated employees)

Gratuity = Last drawn monthly wages × 15 ÷ 26 × completed years of service

A part of a year exceeding six months is counted as a full year. Gratuity is subject to the ceiling notified by the Central Government and must be paid within the prescribed time, failing which interest is payable.

Gratuity can be forfeited only in the cases the law specifies, such as termination for riotous or disorderly conduct, or for an offence involving moral turpitude committed in the course of employment. In Union Bank of India v. C.G. Ajay Babu (2018), the Supreme Court held that forfeiture on the ground of moral turpitude requires that the misconduct actually amounts to such an offence; termination for misconduct alone is not enough.

Maternity benefit and social security

Maternity benefit

  • 26 weeks of paid maternity leave, of which up to eight weeks may be taken before the expected delivery, for a woman with fewer than two surviving children; 12 weeks where she has two or more.
  • 12 weeks for a woman who legally adopts a child below three months of age, and for a commissioning mother.
  • Establishments above the prescribed size must provide a crèche facility. Work from home may be allowed after the leave, by mutual agreement, where the nature of work permits.

Unorganised, gig and platform workers

The Code on Social Security, for the first time, defines gig workers and platform workers and provides for social security schemes for them, funded partly by contributions from aggregators. Unorganised workers can register on the e-Shram portal to access government social security schemes.

Compensation for workplace injury

An employer is liable to pay compensation if an employee suffers personal injury by an accident arising out of and in the course of employment, or contracts a notified occupational disease. No proof of negligence on the employer’s part is required.

  • An accident while commuting between the employee’s residence and the place of employment is deemed to arise out of and in the course of employment, if a connection between the circumstances of the accident and the employment is established (Section 74(4), Code on Social Security).
  • The amount depends on the nature of the injury (death, permanent total or partial disablement, or temporary disablement), the monthly wages and the age of the employee.
  • For employees covered by the Employees’ State Insurance scheme, benefits are generally claimed from the ESI Corporation instead.

Protection against sexual harassment at work

Under the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013, every employer with 10 or more employees must constitute an Internal Committee. Complaints from smaller workplaces, or against the employer, go to the Local Committee constituted by the District Officer. A complaint is to be made within three months of the incident, which the Committee may extend by a further three months for sufficient reason.

Documents to keep ready

  • Appointment order and contract of employment
  • Pay slips and bank statements showing salary
  • Attendance and leave records
  • Identity card or other proof of employment
  • Show-cause notices, charge sheet and inquiry papers
  • Termination or relieving order
  • PF (UAN) and ESI details
  • Medical records and FIR, in injury cases

Key judgments

These decisions were given under the earlier labour laws; their principles continue to guide the interpretation of corresponding provisions in the Codes.

Meaning of “industry”

Bangalore Water Supply & Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa, Supreme Court, 1978

Gave “industry” a wide meaning, covering any systematic activity organised by co-operation between employer and employees for producing goods or services, including hospitals and educational institutions.

Back wages on illegal termination

Deepali Gundu Surwase v. Kranti Junior Adhyapak Mahavidyalaya, Supreme Court, 2013

Where termination is found illegal, reinstatement with continuity of service and back wages is the normal rule, subject to the employer showing that the employee was gainfully employed in the meantime.

Sham contract labour

Steel Authority of India Ltd. v. National Union Waterfront Workers, Supreme Court (Constitution Bench), 2001

Contract labour is not automatically absorbed when contract labour is prohibited. But where the contract is found to be a sham or camouflage, the workers are in fact employees of the principal employer.

Forfeiture of gratuity

Union Bank of India v. C.G. Ajay Babu, Supreme Court, 2018

Gratuity cannot be forfeited for moral turpitude merely because the employee was dismissed for misconduct; the misconduct must constitute an offence involving moral turpitude.

Equal pay for temporary employees

State of Punjab v. Jagjit Singh, Supreme Court, 2016

Temporary employees doing the same work as regular employees are entitled to at least the minimum of the regular pay scale.

Maternity benefit for muster-roll workers

Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Female Workers (Muster Roll), Supreme Court, 2000

Women engaged on a daily-wage or muster-roll basis are entitled to maternity benefit.

Sexual harassment at the workplace

Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan, Supreme Court, 1997

Laid down binding guidelines on preventing sexual harassment at the workplace, later replaced by the POSH Act, 2013.

Components of a fair wage

Workmen v. Reptakos Brett & Co. Ltd., Supreme Court, 1991

Held that the minimum wage should include provision for children’s education, medical needs, recreation and old age, besides food, clothing and shelter.

Priority of provident fund dues

Maharashtra State Co-operative Bank Ltd. v. Assistant Provident Fund Commissioner, Supreme Court, 2009

Provident fund dues have priority over the debts of an employer, including dues to secured creditors such as banks.

No right to strike for government employees

T.K. Rangarajan v. Government of Tamil Nadu, Supreme Court, 2003

Government employees have no fundamental, legal, moral or equitable right to go on strike.
