Motor accident claims (MACT)

A guide to compensation for death, injury and property damage in road accidents under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988: who may claim, the procedure before the Claims Tribunal, how compensation is worked out, and the leading Supreme Court decisions.

Law at a glance

Statute: Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, as amended by the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019. The claims provisions of the amendment came into force on 1 April 2022.
Key provisions: Section 165 (Claims Tribunals), Section 166 (application for compensation), Section 164 (fixed compensation without proof of fault), Section 161 (hit-and-run), Section 162 (golden-hour treatment), Section 134A (Good Samaritans).
Forum: the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal (MACT).

Overview

The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 is beneficial legislation. When a road accident arising out of the use of a motor vehicle causes death, bodily injury or damage to property, the victim, or the family of a deceased victim, can claim compensation from the owner, the driver and the insurer of the vehicle. Claims are decided by Motor Accident Claims Tribunals constituted under Section 165.

A claim before the Tribunal is civil in nature. Its object is just compensation for the loss suffered, not punishment. A criminal case against the driver, if any, proceeds separately, and the outcome of one does not decide the other.

Who can claim

Under Section 166(1), an application for compensation may be made by:

  • the person who has been injured;
  • the owner of the property damaged;
  • all or any of the legal representatives of a person who has died; or
  • an agent duly authorised by the injured person or by the legal representatives.

Where only some of the legal representatives apply, the claim is treated as made on behalf of all of them, and those who have not joined are made respondents.

Where to file

Section 166(2) gives the claimant a choice of Tribunal. The claim may be filed before the Tribunal having jurisdiction over:

  • the area where the accident occurred;
  • the area where the claimant resides or carries on business; or
  • the area where the defendant resides.

Under Section 166(4), the Tribunal is to treat the accident report forwarded to it by the police under Section 159 as an application for compensation. In Gohar Mohammed v. Uttar Pradesh State Road Transport Corporation (2022), the Supreme Court directed that claims be registered on the basis of the First Accident Report and the Detailed Accident Report submitted by the police, so that claims are processed without depending entirely on the victim to file.

Time limit for filing

Section 166(3), introduced by the 2019 amendment and in force from 1 April 2022, says that no application for compensation shall be entertained unless it is made within six months of the accident.

Current position

The validity of Section 166(3) has been challenged before the Supreme Court. By an interim order of 4 November 2025 in ICICI Lombard General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Ayiti Navaneetha, the Court directed that, while the matter is pending, Claims Tribunals and High Courts shall not dismiss claim petitions as time-barred under this provision. When this page was last reviewed, the final decision was awaited. Even so, file as soon as possible after an accident.

Types of claims

1. Claim based on fault (Section 166)

This is the usual claim. The claimant must show that the accident was caused by the negligence of the driver of the vehicle concerned. Negligence need only be shown on a preponderance of probabilities, not beyond reasonable doubt as in a criminal trial (Bimla Devi v. Himachal Road Transport Corporation, 2009). There is no upper limit; the Tribunal awards just compensation on the evidence.

2. Fixed compensation without proof of fault (Section 164)

The owner or insurer is liable to pay a fixed sum of ₹5,00,000 for death or ₹2,50,000 for grievous hurt. The claimant does not have to plead or prove any wrongful act or negligence. This route is quicker but limited to the fixed amount. Whether to claim under Section 164 or Section 166 should be decided on the facts of the case.

3. Hit-and-run accidents (Section 161)

Where the vehicle cannot be identified, compensation of ₹2,00,000 for death and ₹50,000 for grievous hurt is payable under the Compensation to Victims of Hit and Run Motor Accidents Scheme, 2022. This claim is made through the authorities named in the Scheme, not before the Claims Tribunal.

How a claim proceeds

  1. Police report

    The accident is reported to the police, who register the case and investigate. The police are required to send accident reports to the Claims Tribunal and the insurer.

  2. Filing the claim petition

    The claim petition is filed before the competent Tribunal with the supporting documents, naming the driver, the owner and the insurer as respondents.

  3. Response of the respondents

    The owner and the insurer file their written statements. The insurer may raise defences permitted by law, for example that the driver had no valid licence or that the vehicle was used in breach of the policy.

  4. Evidence

    The claimant proves the accident, the negligence, the injuries or death, the age and income of the victim, and the expenses incurred. In injury cases, the doctor who assessed the disability may be examined.

  5. Award

    The Tribunal passes an award fixing the compensation, the persons liable and interest. Compensation is usually deposited by the insurer in the Tribunal and disbursed as the Tribunal directs. Many claims are also settled at Lok Adalats.

How compensation is calculated

In case of death: the multiplier method

For loss of dependency, courts follow the method laid down in Sarla Verma v. Delhi Transport Corporation (2009) and affirmed by the Constitution Bench in National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Pranay Sethi (2017).

Formula

Loss of dependency = (Annual income + future prospects − personal expenses) × multiplier

To this are added the conventional heads: loss of estate, loss of consortium and funeral expenses.

Step 1: Income

The actual income of the deceased, proved by salary records, income-tax returns or other evidence. Where income cannot be proved, the Tribunal assesses a notional income.

Step 2: Future prospects (Pranay Sethi)

  • Married person: one-third if there are 2 to 3 dependants, one-fourth if 4 to 6, and one-fifth if more than 6.
  • Unmarried person: generally one-half.

Step 5: Conventional heads

Pranay Sethi fixed loss of estate at ₹15,000, loss of consortium at ₹40,000 and funeral expenses at ₹15,000, to be increased by 10% every three years. In Magma General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Nanu Ram (2018), the Supreme Court held that consortium includes spousal, parental and filial consortium, so a spouse, children and parents may each be entitled to it.

Illustration

A salaried person in permanent employment, aged 35, earning ₹30,000 a month, leaves a spouse and two children (three dependants).

Annual income ₹3,60,000 + future prospects 50% (₹1,80,000) = ₹5,40,000. Less one-third for personal expenses = ₹3,60,000. Multiplied by 16 = ₹57,60,000 towards loss of dependency, plus the conventional heads.

This is a simplified illustration. Actual awards depend on the evidence and on further heads such as medical expenses incurred before death.

In case of injury

In Raj Kumar v. Ajay Kumar (2011), the Supreme Court listed the heads of compensation in injury cases.

  • Pecuniary: medical and treatment expenses, transport, special diet and attendant charges; loss of earnings during treatment; loss of future earnings because of permanent disability; future medical expenses.
  • Non-pecuniary: pain and suffering; loss of amenities of life; loss of expectation of life; inconvenience and hardship.

For permanent disability, what matters is its effect on the victim’s earning capacity, the functional disability, not merely the percentage of physical disability in the medical certificate.

Documents usually required

  • FIR, scene mahazar and charge sheet or final report
  • Wound certificate, discharge summary and treatment records
  • Original medical bills and receipts
  • Disability certificate issued by a Medical Board
  • Proof of age (Aadhaar, school certificate or similar)
  • Proof of income (salary certificate, pay slips, income-tax returns)
  • Registration, insurance and permit particulars of the vehicle
  • In death cases: death certificate and post-mortem report
  • In death cases: legal heirship certificate and proof of relationship
  • Bank account details of the claimants

Emergency treatment and Good Samaritans

Cashless treatment (Section 162)

The “golden hour” is the one-hour period after a traumatic injury when prompt treatment has the best chance of preventing death (Section 2(12A)). Under the Cashless Treatment of Road Accident Victims Scheme, 2025, framed under Section 162 and in force from 5 May 2025, a road accident victim is entitled to cashless treatment of up to ₹1.5 lakh per person per accident, for up to seven days from the accident, at designated hospitals.

Protection of Good Samaritans (Section 134A)

A person who in good faith and voluntarily gives emergency medical or non-medical help to an accident victim, or takes the victim to hospital, is not liable in any civil or criminal action if the victim is injured or dies because of any act or omission in giving that help. Disclosing one’s name and personal details to the police or hospital is voluntary.

Key judgments

Multiplier and deductions

Sarla Verma v. Delhi Transport Corporation, Supreme Court, 2009

Laid down the age-based multiplier table and the fractions for deducting personal expenses, so that awards in death cases are calculated consistently.

Future prospects and conventional heads

National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Pranay Sethi, Supreme Court (Constitution Bench), 2017

Held that future prospects must be added for the self-employed and those on fixed salaries as well as permanent employees, fixed the percentages, and fixed the amounts for the conventional heads.

Consortium for children and parents

Magma General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Nanu Ram, Supreme Court, 2018

Recognised parental consortium (for children) and filial consortium (for parents) in addition to spousal consortium.

Standard of proof

Bimla Devi v. Himachal Road Transport Corporation, Supreme Court, 2009

Held that negligence in a claim case is decided on a preponderance of probabilities; strict proof as in a criminal trial is not required.

Heads of compensation for injuries

Raj Kumar v. Ajay Kumar, Supreme Court, 2011

Set out the heads of compensation in injury cases and explained how to assess loss of earning capacity from permanent disability.

Value of a homemaker’s work

Kirti v. Oriental Insurance Co. Ltd., Supreme Court, 2021

Recognised the economic value of a homemaker’s work in fixing notional income, and held that future prospects can be added to notional income.

Death of a child

Kishan Gopal v. Lala, Supreme Court, 2013

Applied a notional income to fix compensation for the death of a non-earning child.

Licence defence of the insurer

National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Swaran Singh, Supreme Court, 2004

Held that the insurer must prove a breach of the policy conditions, such as the absence of a valid licence, and that the owner was at fault. Even where the defence succeeds, the insurer may be directed to pay the victim first and recover the amount from the owner.

Light Motor Vehicle licence

Mukund Dewangan v. Oriental Insurance Co. Ltd., Supreme Court, 2017; affirmed in Bajaj Alliance General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Rambha Devi, Supreme Court (Constitution Bench), 2024

A holder of a Light Motor Vehicle licence may drive a transport vehicle whose gross vehicle weight does not exceed 7,500 kg without a separate endorsement.

Liability of the registered owner

Naveen Kumar v. Vijay Kumar, Supreme Court, 2018

The person in whose name the vehicle stands registered remains liable as owner for the purposes of a claim, even if the vehicle had been sold but not transferred in the records.

Registration of claims on police reports

Gohar Mohammed v. Uttar Pradesh State Road Transport Corporation, Supreme Court, 2022

Directed that claims be registered on the basis of the First Accident Report and the Detailed Accident Report forwarded by the police, and laid down time-bound steps for the police, the insurer and the Tribunal.

Frequently asked questions

Can a claim be made if no criminal case was charged against the driver?

Yes. The claim proceeding is independent of the criminal case. The Tribunal decides negligence on the evidence before it, on a preponderance of probabilities.

Can a pillion rider or passenger claim compensation?

A pillion rider or passenger injured because of the negligence of another vehicle’s driver can claim against that vehicle. A claim against the insurer of the vehicle in which the person was travelling depends on the type of policy and its coverage of occupants or pillion riders, for example whether it is an “Act only” or a comprehensive (package) policy.

Is interest payable on the compensation?

Yes. The Tribunal ordinarily awards interest on the compensation from the date of the claim petition until payment, at the rate it fixes.

Is money received under the victim’s own insurance deducted from the compensation?

Amounts received under the deceased’s own life insurance policy are not deducted from compensation under the Motor Vehicles Act (Helen C. Rebello v. Maharashtra State Road Transport Corporation, Supreme Court, 1998). Courts have not taken a uniform view on whether reimbursement received under a personal mediclaim policy can be set off against medical expenses, so the position should be checked for the particular case.
