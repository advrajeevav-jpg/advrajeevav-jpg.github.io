Insurance claims and disputes

What policyholders are entitled to under the insurance laws and IRDAI regulations, the usual grounds on which claims are rejected and what the courts have said about them, and the routes for challenging an unfair rejection.

Law at a glance

Statutes and rules: Insurance Act, 1938; Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Act, 1999; IRDAI regulations and the Master Circular on Protection of Policyholders’ Interests, 2024; Insurance Ombudsman Rules, 2017; Consumer Protection Act, 2019.
Forums: the insurer’s grievance redressal officer, the Insurance Ombudsman, the Consumer Commissions and the civil courts.

Recent change

The Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025 (assented to on 20 December 2025) amends the Insurance Act, 1938. Its provisions take effect on the dates the Central Government notifies. Before relying on a particular section, check whether it has been amended and whether the amendment is in force.

Rights of a policyholder

A contract of insurance is one of utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei). The proposer must disclose all material facts truthfully, and the insurer must deal fairly and explain its terms. Key entitlements include:

  • Free-look period: a new life or health insurance policy may be returned within the free-look period, now 30 days under IRDAI’s 2024 framework, if its terms are not acceptable. The premium is refunded subject to the deductions the regulations allow.
  • Clear information: the insurer must give a Customer Information Sheet setting out the main features, exclusions and claim procedure in simple terms.
  • Reasons for rejection: a claim may be rejected only by a written communication giving specific reasons.
  • Timely settlement: under the 2024 Master Circular, life insurance death claims are to be settled within 15 days of intimation, or within 45 days where investigation is required. Delay beyond the permitted time makes the insurer liable to pay interest.
  • Portability of health insurance: a health policy may be moved to another insurer with credit for waiting periods already completed, as the regulations provide.

Section 45: the three-year rule in life insurance

Under Section 45 of the Insurance Act, 1938, as substituted in 2015:

  • No life insurance policy can be called in question on any ground whatsoever after three years from the date of the policy, the date the risk commenced, the date of revival or the date of a rider, whichever is later.
  • Within those three years, the insurer may repudiate the policy on the ground of fraud, or of a misstatement or suppression of a material fact, but only by communicating the grounds to the insured, or to the nominees or legal representatives, in writing.

After three years, therefore, even an allegation of fraud cannot be used to deny a claim under a life policy.

Nomination and the rights of legal heirs

In Sarbati Devi v. Usha Devi (1984), the Supreme Court held that a nominee under a life insurance policy only receives the money; the legal heirs of the policyholder are entitled to it under the law of succession.

Section 39 of the Insurance Act, as amended in 2015, changed this for close family. Where the nominee is a parent, spouse or child of the policyholder (or the spouse and children together), the nominee is beneficially entitled to the amount, unless the policyholder has provided otherwise. In other cases, the nominee still receives the money on behalf of the legal heirs.

Common grounds of rejection and what the courts have said

Ground of rejectionLegal position
Pre-existing disease Once the insurer has assessed the proposer’s medical condition and issued the policy, it cannot later reject a claim on the basis of a condition the proposer had disclosed (Manmohan Nanda, 2021). Wilful suppression of a material fact remains a valid ground, subject to Section 45 in life insurance.
Delay in informing the insurer In vehicle theft claims where an FIR was lodged promptly, delay in informing the insurer is not by itself a ground to reject a genuine claim (Om Prakash, 2017; Gurshinder Singh, 2020).
Exclusion clauses The insurer must prove that the exclusion applies, and exclusions must be clearly brought to the insured’s notice (Texco Marketing, 2022). An overly broad exclusion of “genetic disorders” was held to be arbitrary (Jai Parkash Tayal, Delhi High Court, 2018).
Ambiguous wording Where the policy terms are ambiguous, they are read against the insurer that drafted them (Pushpalaya Printers, 2004).
Surveyor’s assessment A surveyor’s report is important evidence but not binding or final; it can be departed from for good reason (Pradeep Kumar, 2009).
Refusal to renew A mediclaim policy cannot be refused renewal arbitrarily merely because claims were made under it (Biman Krishna Bose, 2001).

How to challenge a rejection

  1. Written complaint to the insurer

    Make a written complaint to the insurer’s grievance redressal officer, with copies of the rejection letter and the relevant documents. Keep proof of submission. Complaints can also be registered and tracked on IRDAI’s Bima Bharosa grievance portal.

  2. Insurance Ombudsman

    If the insurer rejects the complaint, does not reply within one month, or the reply is unsatisfactory, a complaint may be made to the Insurance Ombudsman within one year. The Ombudsman deals with claims of up to ₹50 lakh, charges no fee and does not require a lawyer. An award, once accepted by the complainant, is binding on the insurer. For Kerala, the Ombudsman’s office is at Kochi.

  3. Consumer Commission

    Alternatively, a complaint for deficiency in service may be filed before the Consumer Commission with pecuniary jurisdiction, within two years of the cause of action. See the consumer protection guide.

  4. Civil court

    A civil suit on the insurance contract is also available, within the period of limitation. It is usually slower than the specialised forums.

Choosing a forum

The Ombudsman will not entertain a complaint on a matter that is already pending before, or has been decided by, a court, Consumer Commission or arbitrator. Choose the forum before filing.

Documents to keep ready

  • Policy schedule, wording and endorsements
  • Proposal form, as submitted
  • Premium receipts
  • Claim form and claim intimation
  • Rejection or settlement letter
  • All correspondence with the insurer or TPA
  • Medical records, discharge summary and bills (health claims)
  • FIR, surveyor’s report and repair estimates (motor and property claims)
  • Death certificate and nominee or heirship documents (life claims)

Key judgments

Exclusion clauses must be clearly disclosed

Texco Marketing Pvt. Ltd. v. TATA AIG General Insurance Co. Ltd., Supreme Court, 2022

An exclusion clause must be specifically brought to the notice of the insured, and the burden of proving that it applies is on the insurer.

Disclosed medical condition

Manmohan Nanda v. United India Assurance Co. Ltd., Supreme Court, 2021

Once a policy is issued after assessing the medical profile disclosed by the proposer, the insurer cannot repudiate a claim by pointing to that existing condition.

Delay in intimation

Om Prakash v. Reliance General Insurance, Supreme Court, 2017; Gurshinder Singh v. Shriram General Insurance Co. Ltd., Supreme Court, 2020

Where the police were informed promptly of a vehicle theft, delay in informing the insurer does not by itself defeat a genuine claim.

Ambiguity read against the insurer

United India Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Pushpalaya Printers, Supreme Court, 2004

Where a policy term is ambiguous, the interpretation that favours the insured is adopted (contra proferentem).

Surveyor’s report not final

New India Assurance Co. Ltd. v. Pradeep Kumar, Supreme Court, 2009

The report of a surveyor is not the last word; the forum may disregard it on cogent grounds.

Renewal of mediclaim

Biman Krishna Bose v. United India Insurance Co. Ltd., Supreme Court, 2001

An insurer cannot arbitrarily refuse to renew a mediclaim policy, for instance only because a claim was made in the previous year.

Nominee and legal heirs

Sarbati Devi v. Usha Devi, Supreme Court, 1984

A nominee receives the policy money but does not become its owner; the heirs take it by succession. For policies governed by Section 39 as amended in 2015, see the note on beneficial nominees above.

Exclusion of genetic disorders

United India Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Jai Parkash Tayal, Delhi High Court, 2018

A broad exclusion of “genetic disorders” was held to be vague and discriminatory, and the regulator was asked to re-examine such exclusions.
